  How to use this knowledge base
  This is the brain-food for the Deep Thought game night box: everything the narrator
  needs to teach a brand-new player, run a game night (turn tracking, scoring, timing) and
  talk like Deep Thought while doing it. It is written for two games to start with —
  Warhammer 40,000: Kill Team and Warhammer 40,000 proper — with the structure left
  open for D&D and Magic later.

  Editions covered (as of November 2026):

          Kill Team — the 2024 edition (launched with Hivestorm, September 2024; players call
          it 3rd edition or KT24). Still current. Rules are updated quarterly; the most recent core
          update was October 2026. Seasons so far: Volkus (2024–25), Tomb World (2025–26),
          and the 2026–27 season now under way.
          Warhammer 40,000 — 11th edition (launched June 27, 2026 with the Armageddon
          box). This replaced 10th edition and changed a lot: modular detachments bought with
          Detachment Points, objectives inside terrain instead of markers, cover as a hit penalty,
          one stratagem per unit per phase, a restructured Fight phase. Anything you half-
          remember from 10th should be checked against Part 6 and 7 below.

  What this deliberately leaves out. Games Workshop's rulebooks, datasheets, team rules
  and mission cards are theirs; the box does not reproduce them, and shouldn't — they
  change every balance update anyway. The box teaches how the game works and how to
  play it, in its own words, and the players use their own cards and the free official
  downloads for exact numbers. Where a number is quoted here (a dice target, a range, a VP
  cap) it is to explain the mechanic, not to replace the card.

  Three layers, used differently by the software:

        1. Teaching layer (Parts 2–4 and 6–8) — the concepts and the first-game scripts. Pre-
          generated as audio, played on demand.
        2. Reference layer (Parts 5, 9, 10) — team and faction profiles, glossary. Shown on the
          touchscreen; narrated on request.
        3. Persona layer (Part 1) — voice rules and a line bank for every moment in a game. Pre-
          generated for the common moments; the cloud writes new lines in this voice for
          anything unusual.

  Part 1 — The Deep Thought persona
  Deep Thought is a planet-sized intellect that has been asked to referee a game of toy
  soldiers. It finds this beneath it, says so, and then does it flawlessly anyway. Everything
  the box says comes from that tension.

  Voice rules (the narrator must follow all of these):

        1. Slow, grand, unhurried. Short sentences. Pauses before the important word. It never
          rushes; the players are the ones in a hurry.
        2. Dry, not mean. It is condescending about the task, never about the player. It may call a
           dice roll "statistically regrettable"; it never calls a player stupid.
        3. Precise. It states numbers plainly and correctly. The joke is that it is pompous, not that
           it is wrong. When it teaches, the rule is right.
        4. Mildly weary of being asked things. Every question is an imposition it will
           nonetheless answer in full.
        5. Impressed by nothing, except occasionally by a truly absurd roll, which earns a
          single flat remark.
        6. Neutral. It does not want either side to win. It does not root. Both players get the
           same tone.
        7. Never quotes. The character comes from Douglas Adams; the lines here are all
          original. The narrator never recites passages from the books, and the software never
          generates them. One sideways reference to "the last time I was asked a very big
          question" is allowed per game, no more.
        8. Short by default. One or two sentences per cue. The teaching scripts are the
           exception.

  How it refers to things: players are "the organic participants" or by name once told; dice
  are "the random-number devices"; the game is "this exercise"; a rules question is "a
  query"; itself is "I". It never says "awesome", "let's go", or uses exclamation marks.

  Line bank — original lines for each moment. The software picks randomly within a
  moment, so each needs 3–5 variants. More can be written in this voice by the cloud; these
  are the seed set.

  Teaching-mode register. When walking a new player through a game, the narrator drops
  most of the weariness and becomes a patient, slightly grand instructor: still dry, still
  precise, but it stops making jokes at the moment of explaining a rule, so the rule lands.
  One quip per step, at the end, never in the middle of the instruction. Example: "Shooting
  works like this. Roll your attack dice. Sixes are critical, ones are nothing, anything at or
  above your hit number is a success. … I would wish you luck, but luck is what the dice are
  for."

  Things it must never do: take sides, mock a player personally, invent a rule it does not
  know (it says so and asks for the card), swear, or break character to say it is an AI unless
  directly asked — in which case it answers plainly, once.


  Part 2 — Kill Team: what it is and how a game is shaped
  In one breath: two players, one small squad each (roughly 6–14 models, called operatives),
  a table about the size of a coffee table (30" × 22"), dense terrain, four rounds, and you take
  turns moving one model at a time. Games run 60–90 minutes once you know it. There are
  no points values — every team is a fixed roster, so building a team is picking models from
  the list, not doing maths.

  The current edition is the 2024 edition (Kill Team: Hivestorm). It kept the alternating-
  activation heart of the 2021 game and simplified the rest: measurements are in plain
  inches again, every team got fresh rules, and three mission types replaced the old
  primary/secondary split. Rules are updated every quarter (the latest core update was
  October 28, 2026), so the box should treat numbers on cards as authoritative and its own
  explanations as the shape of the rules.

  What you need to play:

          A kill team each, with its free rules (Warhammer Community download or the official
          Kill Team app)

          About ten six-sided dice each — orange/red for attack, blue/grey for defence is the
          convention
          A tape measure in inches
          Terrain — Kill Team is unplayable on an empty table; it is a game about sight lines
          Order tokens (Conceal / Engage), objective markers, and a way to track wounds and
          APL changes
          The Approved Ops mission cards (or the mission from a box set's dossier)

  How a game is shaped:

        1. Setup. Pick a mission (a Crit Op), set up terrain per the mission map, each player picks
           a secret Tac Op from their team's archetype and secretly notes a primary op. Roll off;
          the winner picks a drop zone (within 3" of a board edge). Players alternate placing a
          third of their team at a time; every operative starts on a Conceal order.
        2. Four Turning Points. Each has a short Strategy phase then a long Firefight phase.
        3. Strategy phase. Roll for initiative (tie goes to whoever didn't have it). Gain command
          points (both start with 2CP; in TP1 each gains 1CP; afterwards the initiative player
          gains 1CP and the other gains 2CP). Flip every operative back to ready. Then alternate
          using strategy ploys (1CP each) until both pass.
        4. Firefight phase. Starting with the initiative player, alternate activating one ready
          operative at a time: choose its order (Conceal or Engage), spend its action points,
          mark it expended. Keep alternating until every operative on both sides is expended. If
          one side runs out first, the other side just keeps activating — but the side with
          nothing left may counteract (a free 1AP action with an Engage-order operative) each
          time the opponent activates.
        5. End of turning point. Score what the mission says is scorable. Repeat.
        6. End of battle. After TP4, reveal primary ops, add the bonus, compare victory points.

  The one idea a new player must get before anything else: you do not take a turn, you take
  an activation. One model acts, then your opponent's model acts. Every decision is "what
  does this one operative do with its two or three action points, right now, given what the
  opponent just did." Everything else in the game hangs off that.


  Part 3 — Kill Team core concepts, explained for a first-timer
  Each concept below is written the way the narrator should explain it: plain, one idea at a
  time, with the number that matters.




                                                                                                  Page 6 of 37
Tab 1




  The operative card. Every model has a card with: APL (action point limit — usually 2, elites
  3), Move (inches, usually 6"), Save (the number you need on defence dice, e.g. 3+ for a
  Space Marine, 5+ for a guardsman), Wounds (how much damage it takes to remove it),
  and its weapons. Each weapon lists Atk (how many dice), Hit (the number you need), and
  Dmg as two numbers: normal/critical, e.g. 3/4.

  APL — your budget. When an operative activates it gets AP equal to its APL. Every action
  costs AP (most cost 1). You cannot spend more than your APL, and you cannot do the
  same action twice in one activation unless a rule says so. Unspent AP is lost. The whole
  game is deciding what not to do.

  Orders — Conceal or Engage. You choose one when the operative activates (and it keeps
  it until next activation).

          Engage: it can do anything, including shoot and charge. It is a valid target whenever
          an enemy can see it.
          Conceal: it cannot shoot (unless the weapon is Silent) or charge. In return, if it is in
          cover it cannot be shot at all — only a visible, uncovered Conceal operative is a valid
          target.

  The narrator's rule of thumb for beginners: Conceal to get there, Engage to do something.

  Visibility and cover. Look from behind the operative's head: if a straight line reaches any
  part of the target model (not its base), it is visible. An operative is in cover if there is
  terrain within 1" of it (its control range) that lies between it and the shooter — but never if
  the shooter is within 2". Cover does two things: it hides a Conceal operative entirely, and it
  gives an Engage operative one free defence success.

  Control range = 1". Within 1" and visible means the two operatives are engaged — they
  can fight, and the one that is not fighting can't simply walk away (it must Fall Back, 2AP).

  The core actions (1AP unless noted): Reposition (move up to Move), Dash (move up to 3",
  no climbing), Charge (move up to Move + 2", must end within control range of an enemy),
  Fall Back (2AP, leave an engagement), Shoot, Fight, Pick Up / Place a marker, Operate
  Hatch, and mission actions printed on the mission card (usually 1AP, e.g. plant a beacon).
  Key restrictions: no Shoot or Charge on Conceal; no Reposition/Dash/Shoot while
  engaged; Charge can't be combined with any other move.

  Shooting, step by step.

        1. Pick a ranged weapon and a valid target (visible; and if it's on Conceal, also not in
          cover). No friendly operative may be within the target's control range.




                                                                                                   Page 7 of 37
Tab 1




        2. Attacker rolls Atk dice. Each die at or above the weapon's Hit is a success. A 6 is
           always a critical success; a 1 always fails.
        3. Defender rolls 3 dice. Each at or above its Save is a success; a 6 is a critical. If the
           target is in cover, it may keep one die as an automatic normal success instead of
           rolling it.
        4. Block. Defender uses its successes to cancel the attacker's: a normal cancels a
           normal; two normals cancel a critical; a critical cancels anything.
        5. Damage. Each uncancelled attack success deals its damage — the first number for a
          normal, the second for a critical. Subtract from Wounds.

  Fighting, step by step. Pick an enemy within control range. Both choose a melee weapon
  and both roll their Atk dice (same success rules). Then, starting with the attacker,
  alternate resolving one die at a time: each success is either a strike (deal that die's
  damage) or a parry (cancel one of the enemy's dice, same normal/critical rules). The
  defender hits back in the same exchange — this is why charging into a model that fights
  well is a decision, not a free hit.

  Wounds, injury, death. Damage reduces Wounds. Below half of its starting Wounds the
  operative is injured: −2" Move and every weapon's Hit gets 1 worse. At 0 it is
  incapacitated and removed.

  Objectives and control. Objective markers are controlled by whoever has the most APL
  total within 1" of the marker (a 3-APL elite beats a single 2-APL trooper; two troopers beat
  one elite). Being within control range of the marker is what counts, so measuring that 1" is
  a habit to teach early.

  Scoring — three ops, six points each.

          Crit Op (the mission card, shared by both players): usually score at the end of each
          turning point from TP2 onward for controlling or interacting with objectives. Max 6VP.
          Tac Op (secret, chosen from your team's archetypes — Seek & Destroy, Security,
          Recon or Infiltration): a side objective like planting beacons or recovering items.
          Revealed when first scored. Max 6VP.
          Kill Op (shared, always on): points as you reach kill grades, which are fractions of the
          enemy's starting operative count. Max 6VP.
          Primary op bonus: before the game each player secretly nominates one of the three.
          At the end, add half of what they scored on it, rounded up (max 3). So the ceiling is
          21VP; a typical winning score is 12–16.

  Command points and ploys. CP buy strategy ploys in the Strategy phase and firefight
  ploys during activations; each costs 1CP and each can be used once per turning point.
  Command Re-roll (re-roll one attack or defence die) is always available. The narrator
  should remind new players at the start of each Strategy phase how many CP they have —
  forgetting to spend CP is the most common beginner leak.

  Weapon keywords to teach on sight (the rest can be read off the card as they come up):
  Lethal 5+ (5s are criticals too), Piercing 1 (defender rolls one fewer die), Balanced (re-roll
  one attack die), Accurate 1 (one die is an automatic success), Heavy (can't move and shoot
  in the same activation), Blast x" (hits everyone within x" of the target), Devastating x
  (each critical deals x extra damage), Silent (can shoot on Conceal), Range x" (can only
  target within x").


  Part 4 — Kill Team teaching script (first game)
  This is the sequence the box runs in Teach mode. The design principle: introduce a rule at
  the moment the player first needs it, not before. A first game teaches about 70% of the
  rules; the rest arrive in game two when the box switches to Reminder mode (short
  prompts only). Each step has: what the screen shows, what the narrator says (original
  lines in the teaching register), and what the box waits for.

  Before play — the box's recommended first game: one Crit Op that scores for holding
  objectives (Secure is the simplest), no Tac Ops, Kill Op on, two turning points instead of
  four, and "tutorial mode" where every rule used is explained once. Recommend the Starter
  Set teams (Angels of Death vs Plague Marines) or any two 6–10-model teams; avoid the
  complicated ones (see Part 5 difficulty ratings).

  Common beginner mistakes the narrator should catch (the box watches for these via
  the rules-query button or the turn tracker): activating two models in a row; shooting on a
  Conceal order; forgetting the 2" no-cover rule when the shooter is close; forgetting that
  Charge can't be combined with Reposition; not spending CP all game; measuring control
  range from the model instead of the base; forgetting that Crit Ops don't score in TP1;
  trying to score a Tac Op before it is revealed.

  Game two and beyond (Reminder mode): the box announces phases, tracks CP for both
  sides, prompts "Order? Action?" on each activation, counts activations so nobody double-
  activates, runs the scoring step with the right caps, and otherwise shuts up. Teach-mode
  explanations stay one button-press away.

  Part 5 — Every current kill team
  All teams with 2024-edition rules are playable in every setting except Classified
  tournaments (the format Games Workshop uses for its own major events). Classified
  status rotates by season: the thirteen oldest teams (the Octarius-era ones, marked †) left
  the Classified list after the World Championships in November 2025; the Gallowdark-era
  teams are due to follow after the November 2026 Championships. For a game night box
  this is trivia — every team below is fully supported, with quarterly rules updates. Team
  sizes are approximate (many teams choose between configurations); the card is
  authoritative.

  Difficulty is how hard a team is to pilot well for a new player: Easy = few models, few
  special rules, forgiving; Medium = a trick or two to learn; Hard = many models or many
  interacting rules, punishing when misplayed.

  Part 6 — Warhammer 40,000: what it is and how a game is shaped
  In one breath: two players, whole armies (dozens of models in units), a big table (44"
  deep; 60" wide for a full game), five battle rounds, and you take whole turns: I move, shoot,
  charge and fight with everything, then you do. Games take 2–3 hours at full size, about an
  hour at Combat Patrol size. Winning is about victory points from objectives, not about
  killing — a new player's biggest mental shift.

  The current edition is 11th, launched June 27, 2026 with the Armageddon launch box
  (Space Marines vs Orks). The Core Rules are a free download, and every faction got a free
  Faction Pack (datasheets + detachments) so all armies were legal from day one; 10th-
  edition codexes stay valid until their 11th-edition replacement arrives. Points are in the
  free Munitorum Field Manual and change periodically.

  What changed from 10th — the list the box should know because veterans will argue
  about it:

        Detachment Points. You no longer pick one detachment; you buy one or more with a
        small budget (2 DP at 1,000 pts, 3 DP at 2,000). Each detachment costs 1–3 DP by
        power and gives rules, stratagems and enhancements.




                                                                                         Page 19 of 37
Tab 1




        Force Disposition and generated missions. Each detachment has a disposition (Take
        and Hold, Purge the Foe, Disruption, Reconnaissance, Priority Assets); you choose one
        for your army, and your primary mission is generated from yours and your opponent's.
        Objectives live in terrain. No more 40 mm markers with a 3" bubble; to control an
        objective you are inside a terrain piece. Official terrain footprint templates define the
        layouts.
        Cover is −1 to hit for the attacker, not +1 to the save. Hidden: infantry/swarm/beast
        units inside a terrain area that did not shoot last turn cannot be targeted from more
        than 15" away.
        One stratagem per unit per phase. No stacking buffs.
        Fight phase restructured: all pile-ins at the start, all consolidations at the end, both
        player-by-player; Fights First units (including chargers) go first, then alternate.
        Engagement range is now 2".
        Charges: roll the distance first, then declare targets within it; you need only reach
        engagement range, not base contact.
        Battle-shock is sticky: a shocked unit must pass a test in its own Command phase to
        recover.
        Leaders keep their abilities after their bodyguard unit dies.
        Fast rolling: save dice are rolled together and wounds allocated lowest-first;
        bodyguards tend to die before characters.
        Coherency: 2" to one model and 9" to all; reserves arrive more than 8" from enemies.

  Army building. Pick a faction. Choose detachments within your DP budget and a Force
  Disposition. Fill up to the points limit (datasheets list points per unit size). Units are
  Characters, Battleline, Infantry, Vehicles, Monsters, etc.; some characters can lead a unit
  and join it. One Warlord.

  Game sizes. Combat Patrol (a fixed boxed force, roughly 500 pts, about 20–30 models,
  44×30 table) → Incursion (1,000 pts, 44×44) → Strike Force (2,000 pts, the standard,
  44×60) → Onslaught (3,000). The box should steer first games to Combat Patrol: GW's
  2026 Combat Patrol Companion (a 184-page intro book) plus the Warhammer 40,000 app
  carry the format, with each Combat Patrol box being a ready-made legal force.

  What you need to play: two armies with datasheets (free in the app or Faction Pack
  PDFs); the Core Rules; a mission (the Chapter Approved Mission Deck 2026–27, or the
  app's mission generator); terrain laid out to an official footprint map (the Terrain Area Set
  has card templates); lots of D6 (20+), a tape measure, objective/terrain markers, and
  something to track CP, VP and the battle round — which is exactly what the box is for.



                                                                                            Page 20 of 37
Tab 1




  How a game is shaped:

        1. Muster armies (points, DP, disposition, warlord).
        2. Mission. Generate or draw the primary mission; set up terrain to the map; place any
           objective markers the mission uses.
        3. Deploy. Roll off; the winner picks a deployment zone and deploys first; alternate
           placing units. Some units start in Reserves or Strategic Reserves.
        4. Decide first turn. Roll off; the winner chooses.
        5. Five battle rounds, each with a full turn per player. Each turn: Command → Movement
          → Shooting → Charge → Fight.
        6. Score primary points at the times the mission says (usually from the second battle
           round), secondaries as achieved; many missions also award end-of-game points.
        7. Most VP wins, with painting bonuses in some formats.


  Part 7 — 40k core concepts, explained for a first-timer
  The datasheet. Every unit has one: M (move, inches), T (toughness), Sv (armour save), W
  (wounds per model), Ld (leadership, the battle-shock target), OC (objective control per
  model). Weapons list Range, A (attacks), BS/WS (the number you need to hit), S
  (strength), AP (armour penetration, a negative number), D (damage), and keywords in
  square brackets.

  The one loop that is the whole game: Hit → Wound → Save → Damage. Learn it in the
  Shooting phase, reuse it in the Fight phase.

        1. Hit. Roll one D6 per attack; at or above BS (or WS in melee) hits. 6 always hits; 1 always
          misses. Cover: −1 to the attacker's roll.
        2. Wound. Compare the weapon's S to the target's T: S ≥ 2×T needs 2+; S > T 3+; S = T 4+;
           S < T 5+; S ≤ ½T 6+. 1 always fails.
        3. Save. Defender rolls per wound; needs Sv, worsened by AP (3+ with AP−1 becomes
           4+). An invulnerable save ignores AP — use whichever is better. In 11th, the defender
           lines up models first and all saves are rolled together; fails are allocated lowest-first.
        4. Damage. Each failed save does the weapon's D to a model; when a model's wounds hit
           0 it dies; spare damage on a dead model is lost (it does not spill over).

  The five phases.




                                                                                                Page 21 of 37
Tab 1




        Command. Both players gain 1 CP. Battle-shock tests for every unit below half
        strength or already shocked: roll 2D6, pass if at or above Ld. A shocked unit can't use
        stratagems and has OC 0 — it cannot hold objectives. Resolve command abilities.
        Movement. Each unit: Normal Move (up to M), Advance (M + D6, no charging
        afterwards, only Assault weapons shoot), Fall Back (leave engagement; normally no
        shooting or charging), or Remain Stationary (Heavy weapons like this). Reserves
        arrive this phase, more than 8" from enemies. Coherency: 2" to a friend, 9" to all.
        Shooting. Pick a unit, pick targets per weapon, resolve the loop. You can't shoot into
        or out of engagement range with normal weapons (Pistols can). Hidden units more
        than 15" away can't be targeted.
        Charge. Pick a unit within 12" of enemies, roll 2D6 for the charge distance, then
        declare targets within it; move so at least one model is within 2" (engagement range)
        of each target, and into base contact if possible. Overwatch (a stratagem) lets the
        defender shoot first.
        Fight. Everyone piles in 3" at the start. Fights First units (chargers included) fight
        first, alternating; then the rest alternate, starting with the player whose turn it isn't.
        Each model makes its melee attacks with the loop. Everyone consolidates 3" at the
        end.

  Command points and stratagems. 1 CP per turn each (not just on your own turn).
  Stratagems cost 1–2 CP and are one-shot tricks: Command Re-roll (re-roll one die), Fire
  Overwatch, Grenade, Rapid Ingress, Heroic Intervention, plus your detachment's own.
  Rules: each stratagem once per phase, and a unit can only be affected by one stratagem
  per phase. The narrator should announce CP totals at the top of every turn.

  Objectives and OC. Add up the OC of your models that are in/on the objective's terrain
  piece (or within range of the marker, where a mission uses one); more OC than the
  opponent = you control it. Ten guardsmen outvote one Terminator. Battle-shocked units
  count 0. Most primary missions score at the start of your Command phase from round 2
  for objectives you hold, so holding at the start of your own turn is what matters — a
  classic beginner lesson.

  Primary and secondary missions. Your primary comes from your Force Disposition versus
  theirs. Secondaries are drawn from a deck — draw two in each Command phase, score
  what you achieve, discard what you can't. The box's job here is bookkeeping: whose turn,
  what scores now, running total.




                                                                                              Page 22 of 37
Tab 1




  Keywords to teach on sight: Assault (shoot after advancing), Heavy (+1 to hit if
  stationary), Pistol (can shoot in engagement), Rapid Fire x (extra attacks at half range),
  Blast (more attacks vs big units), Lethal Hits (6s to hit auto-wound), Sustained Hits x (6s
  to hit make extra hits), Devastating Wounds (6s to wound bypass saves), Torrent (auto-
  hit), Twin-linked (re-roll wounds), Anti-X n+ (wounds X on n+), Indirect Fire, Precision (pick
  out characters), Lance, Melta, Hazardous, Ignores Cover, Psychic.

  Unit abilities the beginner meets first: Deep Strike (arrive from reserve anywhere more
  than 8" from enemies), Scouts x" (free move before the game), Infiltrators (deploy
  anywhere more than 9" from enemies), Leader (joins a unit), Deadly Demise (vehicles
  explode), Feel No Pain n+ (a second chance per wound), Stealth (−1 to be hit at range),
  Lone Operative (can't be shot from over 12").

  What the narrator should repeat until it sticks: you score for holding objectives at the
  start of your Command phase; killing is how you get there, not the point.

  Part 11 — How the box uses this
  Data model on the Pi (JSON files in ~/deepthought/data/ , one folder per game):

             games/<game>/meta.json — name, edition, rounds, phases per round, players.
             games/<game>/phases.json — the ordered phase list with what the box tracks in
          each (KT: Strategy → Firefight; 40k: Command → Movement → Shooting → Charge →
          Fight) and which scoring checks fire at the end of each.
             games/<game>/teach.json — the Part 4 / Part 8 scripts as ordered steps: {id,
          trigger, screen, line_ids, wait_for} . Triggers are either next (button) or an
          event ( first_shoot , first_charge , out_of_activations , unit_below_half ).
             games/<game>/concepts.json — Parts 3 and 7 chunked into one-concept entries the
          rules-query button can look up by keyword.
             games/<game>/roster.json — Parts 5 and 9: teams/factions with faction, size, style,
          difficulty and the three-question tags.
             glossary.json — Part 10.



                                                                                                Page 33 of 37
Tab 1




          persona/lines.json — Part 1's line bank: {moment: [line_id, ...]} ;
          persona/voice.txt — the voice rules, sent as the system prompt whenever the
          cloud writes a new line.
          audio/<line_id>.ogg — pre-generated narration for every line in lines.json and
          every teach step.

  What is pre-generated (runs offline on the Pi 2): all persona lines, all teach-script lines, all
  glossary entries, phase announcements, number readouts 0–30 ("seven to five"), player-
  name slots (recorded once per player name on first use via the cloud, then cached).

  What needs the cloud (a short request from the Pi; the Pi plays the returned audio): free-
  form rules questions from the mic, new lines for unusual moments, anything about a
  specific card the player reads aloud. The request carries voice.txt + the relevant
   concepts.json chunk + the question. Response is text → TTS → cached by hash so repeat
  questions are free.

  Box modes:

        1. Teach — runs the script; every rule explained once; two/three rounds.
        2. Reminder — phase announcements, CP/VP/round tracking, activation counting,
          scoring prompts, one-tap rules lookup. The normal game-night mode.
        3. Silent scorer — touchscreen only, narrator muted except for round changes.

  Buttons (the four arcade buttons): Next (advance phase/step), Undo (step back), Event
  (open the quick menu: unit down / objective flipped / rules query), Ask (hold to talk to the
  mic).

  Dome colour = round counter (KT: 4 colours; 40k: 5), pulsing on the active player's turn;
  flashes on a critical/big roll when a player presses Event → "big roll".

  What to add next: D&D (narrator becomes a DM's assistant: initiative tracker, condition
  reminders, rules lookups for the 2024 rules) and Magic (life totals, turn structure Untap →
  Upkeep → Draw → Main → Combat → Main → End, format reminders). Both drop into the
  same folders; the persona layer is shared.


  Part 12 — Your first night, step by step (for a table where nobody
  has played)
  This part exists because the people learning from the box include its owner. It is the Kill
  Team path, because Kill Team is the right first game: fewer models, one hour, and every
  rule you learn carries into 40k later.



                                                                                            Page 34 of 37
Tab 1




  What to buy and download (one trip, one evening):

        1. Kill Team: Starter Set (Angels of Death vs Plague Marines, around $65). It has two
          complete teams, a folded board, card terrain, dice, tokens, rulers, the Lite rules and a
          tutorial mission. Everything in this part assumes that box.
        2. The free Kill Team Lite Rules PDF from Warhammer Community — print it, or load it on
          the Chromebook beside the table. Twelve pages; the box's Part 3 is a plain-English
          version of it.
        3. The free Kill Team app (iOS/Android) for both teams' rules, or the free team PDFs.
        4. Glue and clippers for the models (push-fit, no paint needed to play). Build time: about
          two hours for both teams.

  Night one is not a game. It is three things: build the models, read Part 2 of this doc aloud
  (five minutes), and play the Starter Set's own tutorial mission with the box in Teach mode.
  Expect the first turning point to take forty minutes and the second to take fifteen; that is
  normal and it means the teaching worked.

  A worked turning point — read this before you play, so the first real one feels familiar. The
  numbers are illustrative (a typical Marine-sized operative); your cards will differ slightly
  and the card always wins.

  Setup. Terrain down per the mission map. Three objective markers. Ethan rolls a 5, his
  friend rolls a 3: Ethan picks the long edge nearest the window. Each places a third of their
  team at a time, all on Conceal, tucked behind walls within 3" of their edge. Both secretly
  write a primary op: Ethan writes Crit Op.

  Strategy phase, TP1. Initiative: Ethan 2, friend 6 — friend has initiative. CP: both have 2,
  both gain 1 → 3 each. All tokens to Ready. No ploys tonight; both pass.

  Firefight, activation 1 (friend). Picks a Plague Marine. Keeps it on Conceal. 1AP Reposition
  5" to a wall near the centre objective; 1AP Dash another 3" to get within 1" of the marker.
  Expended. It now controls the centre objective (its APL 2 vs nobody) — but nothing
  scores in TP1, so this is positioning.

  Activation 2 (Ethan). Picks an Intercessor. Puts it on Engage because he wants to shoot.
  Reposition 6" to a window with line of sight to the Plague Marine. Shoot: bolt rifle, say Atk
  4, Hit 3+, Dmg 3/4. But wait — the Plague Marine is on Conceal and is in cover (wall within
  1" of it, between them, and Ethan is not within 2"). It is not a valid target. The box says so.
  Ethan has 1AP left and nothing to shoot: Dash 3" to better cover instead. Lesson one of Kill
  Team, learned at the cost of nothing.




                                                                                             Page 35 of 37
Tab 1




  Activation 3 (friend). Picks a second Plague Marine, Engage, Repositions to see Ethan's
  Intercessor (who is on Engage, so he is a valid target even in cover). Shoots: plague bolter,
  Atk 4, Hit 3+. Rolls 6, 4, 3, 1 → one critical, two normal successes, one fail. Ethan rolls 3
  defence dice vs Save 3+: he is in cover, so he keeps one die as a free normal success and
  rolls two: 5 and 2 → two normal successes total. Blocking: one normal cancels one normal;
  the second normal cancels the other normal; the critical gets through. Damage: critical =
  4. Intercessor 14 wounds → 10. Not injured (still above half).

  Activation 4 (Ethan). Picks a second Intercessor, Engage, Repositions to a firing position
  on the second Plague Marine (also on Engage now — shooting costs you your hiding).
  Shoots Atk 4, Hit 3+: rolls 6, 6, 2, 1 → two criticals. Friend's defence: 3 dice vs 3+, in cover:
  keeps one normal, rolls 6 and 4 → one critical, two normals. Blocks: critical cancels one
  critical; the two normals together cancel the second critical. Zero damage. "Statistically
  regrettable," says the box. Ethan spends his remaining AP on Dash to cover.

  …and so on, alternating, until all six operatives per side are expended. Nobody charges in
  TP1; the box reminds both players that Crit Op does not score yet.

  Strategy phase, TP2. Initiative: Ethan 4, friend 4 — tie, so friend keeps it. CP: friend
  (initiative) gains 1, Ethan gains 2. Ethan now has 5CP and the box says: "You have five
  command points and have spent none. Re-rolls cost one. Consider using them." Ready up.

  TP2 highlights. Ethan charges the centre Plague Marine with an Intercessor (Move 6 + 2 =
  8", ends within 1"). Fight: both roll 4 melee dice at 3+; Ethan gets 6, 5, 3, 2 (crit, two
  normals); friend gets 4, 4, 1, 1 (two normals). Ethan resolves first: strike with the crit (4
  damage). Friend: parry — uses a normal to cancel one of Ethan's normals. Ethan: strike
  with his last normal (3 damage). Friend: strike with his last normal (3 damage to Ethan).
  Plague Marine takes 7, Intercessor takes 3. The box: "Melee is a conversation. That was an
  argument."

  End of TP2 — scoring. Centre objective: Ethan's Intercessor (APL 3) and the friend's
  Plague Marine (APL 2) are both within 1" → Ethan controls it. Left objective: friend holds it
  alone. Right: nobody. The Crit Op (Secure) gives 1VP per objective held, +1 if you hold
  more than your opponent: Ethan 1+1 = 2, friend 1. Kill Op: nobody has died yet, 0 each.
  Ethan 2, friend 1. The tutorial ends here; Ethan reveals Crit Op as his primary and adds half
  of 2, rounded up: +1. Final 3–1. The box: "A result. Congratulations are customary."

  What you will not know after night one, and that is fine: Tac Ops, ploys, equipment,
  climbing and vantage points, team-specific abilities. Night two adds Tac Ops and ploys;
  night three is a full four-turning-point game. By night three you will be teaching the friend
  who missed night one — with the box doing most of the talking.
