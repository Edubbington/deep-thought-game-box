  On the bench

        Five printed parts: riser, base plate, core, head bell, chin plate
        Pi 2 + microSD card, 5" DSI display with its ribbon and screws
        Amp + speaker kit, LED ring, one 30 mm arcade button
        Wi-Fi nano, USB mic (optional)
        5 V 3 A power supply, panel-mount micro-USB extension
        Jumper wires, screw kit, six 6 × 3 mm magnets, foam tape, zip ties, ballast (washers or
        coins)

  Tools: soldering iron, small Phillips screwdriver, flush cutters, hobby knife, sandpaper,
  super glue, epoxy, drill with 3 mm and 2 mm bits.

  Part 2 — Prep

  Step 2.1 — Clean the prints
     1. Remove supports and brims.
     2. Run a knife around every hole and slot to clear strings.
     3. Lightly sand the top of the core and the flat underside of the chin plate.


  Step 2.2 — Trim the riser's top edge


                  Image — Riser interior: ballast trays, zip-tie anchors, power-jack slot; top edge trimm…



     1. Cut off what is left of the thin failed lip along the top of the riser wall.
     2. Sand the wall top flat all the way round.

  Check: the base plate drops onto the riser and does not slide.


  Step 2.3 — Test-fit the chin plate on the core


                 Image — Chin plate lifted above the core: the core's wall, its magnet pocket, and the w…

                Image — Underside of the chin plate: three channels, three magnet pockets, wire opening



     1. Find the 2 mm wall on top of the core. It is in three pieces: front edge, left edge, one
        diagonal.
     2. Find the three matching channels on the flat underside of the chin plate.
     3. Lower the chin plate so each wall piece drops into its channel.

  Check: the chin plate sits flat on the core and does not rock. It only fits one way round.


                          Image — Cross-cut: the core's wall standing inside the chin plate's channel



  Seated: the wall stands in its channel with 0.4 mm of room on each side and on top.


  Step 2.4 — Magnets
     1. Stack all six magnets. Draw a line across the top of the stack with a marker. Peel them
        apart.
     2. Glue three into the chin plate's pockets, marked side in, with a drop of epoxy.
     3. Glue three into the core's top pockets, marked side out.
     4. Press each one flush, wipe the excess, and let it cure.

  Check: once cured, the chin plate snaps down onto the core.


  Step 2.5 — Test-fit the parts
  Nothing is fastened yet. You are only checking that each part goes where it should.

        Button into the 28 mm hole at the front of the base plate.
        Power jack into the slot in the riser's back wall.
        Speaker into the slot on the inside right wall of the bell.
        Display into the pocket behind the window, glass facing out.

  The display is about 8 mm thick and the pocket is 8 mm deep, so its back should sit level
  with the rim. It is held by three printed clamp tabs (Step 6.2).

  Part 3 — The riser

  Step 3.1 — Power jack


                                    Image — Riser back wall with the 14 × 9 mm power-jack slot



     1. Hold the jack in the slot from the inside, port facing out.
     2. Mark its two ear holes on the wall. Drill them 3 mm.
     3. Fasten the jack with two M3 screws.
     4. Leave its cable coiled inside the riser.


  Step 3.2 — Ballast


                  Image — Riser with the micro-USB jack in the back-wall slot and ballast in the two trays



     1. Fill the two floor trays with washers or coins, about 100–150 g in total.
     2. Glue them in with a bead of glue so they cannot rattle.
     3. Loop a loose zip tie through each of the two floor anchors.


  Part 4 — The base plate

  Step 4.1 — Button leads


               Image — Back of the button with the two switch tabs and the LED + and LED – tabs labell…



  Safety check at first power-up: the button's light should come on before you press it. If it is
  dark, do not press the button; unplug and check which tabs the blue wires are on.

     1. Take four jumper wires and cut one connector off each, keeping the wire full length.
        Each lead must reach from the button to the Pi in the head, about 35 cm; if your
        jumpers are shorter, solder two end to end.
     2. Solder two to the switch tabs (COM and NO).
     3. Solder two to the LED tabs (+ and –).

     4. Label them with tape: SW, SW, LED+, LED–.


  Step 4.2 — Button and LED ring


               Image — Base plate with the LED ring centred on the wire hole and the arcade button thr…



  Prepare the ring's leads first. The ring has two 3-wire leads soldered to its back. Read the
  tiny labels on the pads: the lead on the pads marked DI (or DIN) is the input and is the one
  you use. Cut the plastic connector off that lead and solder each wire to a purple jumper
  that has had one connector cut off: red (5V), green (DI), black (GND). Cover each joint
  with heat-shrink. The other lead, on the pads marked DO, is not used: leave its connector
  on and tuck it down the wire hole.

     1. Push the button through the front hole from above. Tighten its nut from below.
     2. Set the LED ring on the flat top, LEDs up, centred on the 30 mm wire hole. The plate
        has no recess for it.
     3. Stick the ring down with three small squares of foam tape.


  Step 4.3 — Bring the leads up through the wire hole


                   Image — Base plate cut in half, seen from below: the hollow underside and button hole



     1. Run the four button leads under the plate and up through the wire hole.
     2. Bring the power cable's plug up through the same hole.
     3. Set the plate on the riser: button at the front, power jack at the back. Do not glue it;
        the ring under the plate holds it in place.

  Check: three things stick up through the middle of the LED ring: button leads, ring leads,
  power plug.

  Part 5 — The core

  Step 5.1 — Thread the wires up the neck


                Image — Core cut in half: the neck channel runs from the top plate down into the hollow…



     1. Drop a piece of string down the 14 mm channel in the top of the core.
     2. Tie the power plug to it and pull the plug up through, from the dome end to the top.
     3. Pull the ring leads and button leads through the same way, a couple at a time.


  Step 5.2 — Set the core on the plate


                      Image — Core from above: magnet pockets, wall pieces, neck-channel exit, dome



     1. Lower the core so the dome is centred over the LED ring.
     2. Take up the slack in the leads as you lower it, so nothing is trapped under the dome's
        rim.
     3. Hold it in place with two small squares of foam tape under the feet. Do not glue it yet.

  The core has no pegs or sockets. It sits flat on the plate (the dome's rim and two feet) and
  is glued down in Part 10, after the LED test shows the glow is centred.

  Step 6.1 — Pi onto the display


                  Image — Back of the 5 inch DSI display, labelled: four Pi standoffs, ribbon socket, bac…



  Your display, seen from the back. The dashed outline is where the Pi sits.

     1. Lay the display face down, the way it is in the photo. Check the Backlight switch is on.
     2. Set the Pi on the four brass standoffs, chips facing you: SD card end toward the
        ribbon socket, USB ports toward the backlight switch. It only fits this way round; the
        other way it hangs off the edge.
     3. Fasten it with the four screws from the display's box.
     4. Push one end of the ribbon into the display's ribbon socket and the other into the Pi's
        DISPLAY socket (lift each latch, slide in, press closed).
     5. Leave the 2-pin fan header empty. The display gets its power through the ribbon;
        there are no power leads to connect.


  Step 6.2 — Display into the bell


                 Image — Section through the loaded bell, right side: display glass against the window l…



  Which way up: put the display in with its backlight-switch edge at the bottom (toward
  the open bottom of the bell), so the printed text on its back is upside down. That points
  the Pi's power socket down toward the neck, where the power cable comes from, and
  keeps the USB ports away from the speaker. If the picture is upside down at first boot,
  Step 9.6 flips it.

     1. Pass the display and Pi in through the open bottom, glass toward the window.
     2. Push it forward into the pocket until the glass meets the lip.
     3. Find the three small screw holes in the rim beside the pocket: two on the left, one at
        the top right. (The lower right has none; the speaker slot is there.) Screw a printed
        clamp tab (DeepThought_5_DisplayTabs.stl) onto each with an M3 screw 8 to 10 mm
        long, long end pointing over the display. Just snug.

  Step 6.3 — Speaker


                   Image — Head bell cut open, right half: speaker slot, side zip-tie slots, wiring pad fo…



  The speaker is held by two small printed blocks
  (DeepThought_6_SpeakerEarBlocks_v3.stl; the file has four, two are spares). Each block
  sits on the front of one ear and is screwed to the grille from outside.


                 Image — Top-down diagram: speaker behind the grille wall, a printed block in front of e…



     1. Hold a block against the front of one ear (the cone side), flat face on the ear, slanted
        edges facing forward, hugging the side of the speaker body.
     2. From behind the ear, drive an M2.5 × 8 screw through the ear hole into the block hole
        next to the speaker body. Repeat for the other ear.
     3. Tilt the speaker and work it up into the slot, cable side down, cone toward the grille
        holes, the same way it goes in on its own.
     4. From outside the bell, find the middle row of grille holes (the longest row). Push an
        M2.5 × 8 screw through the second hole from each end of that row into the block
        behind it. Snug, not tight.
     5. Lead the speaker wire toward the back wall.


  Step 6.4 — Amp, Wi-Fi and mic


                 Image — Section through the loaded bell, left side: USB stack with Wi-Fi nano and mic, …



     1. Plug the speaker into the amp.
     2. Stick the amp to the pad on the back wall with foam tape, pins toward the Pi.
     3. Plug the kit's cable into the amp.
     4. Plug the Wi-Fi nano and the mic into the Pi's USB ports.

  Step 6.5 — Wire the amp
  The amp's own cable ends in five female connectors that push straight onto the Pi; no
  jumpers needed. Follow each wire back to the label printed on the amp, then: G (black) to
  pin 9, V (red) to pin 4, BCL (blue) to pin 12, LRC (green) to pin 35, DIN (yellow) to pin 40.
  Go by the printed labels if your wire colours differ. Leave the leads from the base for Part
  8.


  Part 7 — Wiring
  Pin numbers are physical pin numbers. Pin 1 is the corner pin nearest the SD card slot; odd
  numbers are the inner row.

  The 5 V splice. The amp takes 5 V from pin 4. The LED ring and the button LED share pin 2:
  twist together the bare top ends of the ring's 5 V lead and the button's LED + lead with a
  short stub of jumper that still has its female connector, solder and cover with heat-shrink.
  The stub plugs onto pin 2.

  Before power: nothing on pin 1 or pin 17, and no 5 V lead on any numbered GPIO pin. That
  is the one mistake that kills a Pi.

        Ring: data goes to the pad marked DIN, not DOUT.
        Amp: leave GAIN and SD unconnected.
        Button LED: lit whenever the box is on. If it stays dark, swap its two leads.

  Part 9 — Software
  You do this on the Chromebook or any computer with an SD card slot, before the first
  power-up. The Pi 2 is 32-bit only, so pick the 32-bit images throughout.

  Step 9.1 — Flash the card. Install Raspberry Pi Imager from raspberrypi.com/software.
  Choose Raspberry Pi 2 as the device, Raspberry Pi OS Lite (32-bit) as the OS, and your SD
  card as the storage. Before writing, click Edit settings: set the hostname to deepthought ,
  a username and password, your Wi-Fi network and country, and on the Services tab turn
  on SSH with password authentication. Write the card.

  Step 9.2 — Edit config.txt. When Imager finishes, pop the card out and back in; a drive
  called bootfs appears. Open config.txt in a text editor and add these lines at the end:

     # Deep Thought game night box
     dtparam=audio=off
     dtoverlay=hifiberry-dac
     dtparam=spi=on
     display_auto_detect=1


  The first two switch the Pi's built-in headphone audio off and route I2S to the amp.
  spi=on enables the pin the LED ring uses. The display line lets the DSI panel enumerate
  itself; the Hosyond 5" needs no extra driver on current Raspberry Pi OS. Save, eject the
  card and put it in the Pi.

  Step 9.3 — First connection. Power the box on. Wait about two minutes for the first boot
  to resize the filesystem and join Wi-Fi. From the Chromebook's terminal (or any SSH
  client), connect with ssh <your-user>@deepthought.local . If .local doesn't resolve,
  find the Pi's IP address in your router's device list.

  Step 9.4 — Update and install the basics.

     sudo apt update && sudo apt full-upgrade -y
     sudo apt install -y python3-pip python3-pygame python3-gpiozero python3-
     spidev alsa-utils git
     sudo pip3 install --break-system-packages pi5neo adafruit-circuitpython-
     neopixel-spi

   pygame is the lightweight UI that will draw the touchscreen screens (a browser is too
  heavy for the Pi 2). gpiozero reads the buttons. neopixel-spi drives the ring over SPI
  pin 19 without needing root or the PWM pin.

  Step 9.5 — Point audio at the amp. Run aplay -l . You should see a card named
  sndrpihifiberry . Make it the default:

     sudo tee /etc/asound.conf > /dev/null <<'EOF'
     pcm.!default { type hw card sndrpihifiberry }
     ctl.!default { type hw card sndrpihifiberry }
     EOF


  Step 9.6 — Touch input and screen rotation. The Hosyond panel reports touch as a
  standard USB-HID-style input, so pygame sees finger taps as mouse clicks with nothing
  extra. If the picture is upside down relative to how the panel sits in the head, add
   display_lcd_rotate=2 to config.txt and reboot; touch follows the rotation.

  Step 9.7 — Make it start on boot. Create a folder ~/deepthought , drop the game app
  there (we'll build it together next), and add a systemd service so it runs at power-on
  without a login:

     sudo tee /etc/systemd/system/deepthought.service > /dev/null <<'EOF'
     [Unit]
     Description=Deep Thought game night box
     After=multi-user.target
     [Service]
     User=<your-user>
     WorkingDirectory=/home/<your-user>/deepthought
     ExecStart=/usr/bin/python3 main.py
     Restart=on-failure
     Environment=SDL_VIDEODRIVER=kmsdrm
     [Install]
     WantedBy=multi-user.target
     EOF
     sudo systemctl enable deepthought


  Leave it disabled (skip the last line) until the hardware tests in Part 10 all pass.


  Part 10 — First boot and tests
  Run these in order over SSH. Each one proves one subsystem, so a failure points straight
  at the part to check.


                                                                                           Page 13 of 18
Deep Thought Game Night Box — Assembly Guide




  Test 1 — Power. Plug the PSU into the panel jack and flip its switch. The Pi's red PWR LED
  should come on solid and the green ACT LED should flicker within a few seconds. Run
   vcgencmd get_throttled once logged in; throttled=0x0 means the supply is good.
  Anything with 0x50000 in it means under-voltage: shorten or thicken the micro-USB
  extension, or check the jack's screws aren't pinching the cable.

  Test 2 — Display. The console text should appear on the 5" panel during boot. If it is black,
  see Troubleshooting. Run ls /dev/input/ and tap the screen; a new event* device with
   evtest (install with sudo apt install evtest ) should print coordinates when you
  touch.

  Test 3 — Sound. speaker-test -c 1 -t sine -f 440 -l 2 plays two seconds of an A
  note through the grille. Then aplay /usr/share/sounds/alsa/Front_Center.wav plays a
  voice sample. Nothing? Confirm aplay -l lists sndrpihifiberry , then re-seat the amp's
  BCLK/LRC/DIN jumpers.

  Test 4 — LED ring. Save this as ringtest.py and run it with python3 ringtest.py :

     import time, board, neopixel_spi as neopixel
     ring = neopixel.NeoPixel_SPI(board.SPI(), 16, brightness=0.3,
     auto_write=False)
     for i in range(16):
            ring.fill((0,0,0)); ring[i] = (0, 120, 255); ring.show();
     time.sleep(0.08)
     ring.fill((40, 0, 80)); ring.show(); time.sleep(2)
     ring.fill((0,0,0)); ring.show()


  One blue LED should chase around the ring and then the whole ring should glow purple
  under the dome. Wrong colours or random flicker: add the level shifter or shorten the data
  lead.

  Now glue the core. With the ring lit, turn the core until the glow is even all round the
  dome. Mark the feet's position on the plate with a pencil, lift the core, run super glue
  around the dome's rim and under both feet, and set it back on the marks. Hold for a
  minute. Keep glue away from the ring.

  Test 5 — Button. Save as buttontest.py and run it with python3 buttontest.py :

     from gpiozero import Button
     from signal import pause
     btn = Button(17, pull_up=True, bounce_time=0.05)
     btn.when_pressed = lambda: print("pressed")
     btn.when_released = lambda: print("released")
     print("Press the button (Ctrl-C to quit)")

  Each press prints once. Nothing prints: check one switch lead is on pin 11 and the other on
  pin 20. Prints twice per press: raise bounce_time to 0.1.

  Test 6 — Wi-Fi and reboot. ping -c 3 raspberrypi.com confirms the nano Wi-Fi is up.
  Then sudo reboot ; when it comes back and you can SSH in again without touching
  anything, the box is finished. Enable the service from Step 9.7 once the game software is
  on it.

  Done. Put the magnets' spare set in the parts drawer, snap a photo of the header wiring
  for later, and set the box on the table. The next conversation is the software: the narrator,
  the game modes, and the touchscreen screens.
