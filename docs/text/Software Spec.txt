  1 — What it is and what it can do
  Deep Thought is a Python program that runs at boot on the Pi 2 inside the printed body. It
  owns the 5" touchscreen, the one big arcade button on the base, the LED ring in the
  dome, the speaker and the mic. It has one job with four faces: be the game night's referee,
  teacher, encyclopedia and voice, in the character of a planet-sized intellect mildly put out
  at being asked.

  The one button. The touchscreen does everything, but dice hands and a 5" screen don't
  mix mid-game, so the button covers the three things players do constantly: short press =
  Next (advance the phase / step / activation), hold = talk (push-to-talk to ask a question;
  release to send), double-tap = Undo (step back one event). Its LED glows steadily, pulses
  when it is the player's move, and goes bright while listening.

  Encyclopedia — "ask it anything Warhammer"

         Factions and armies: who they are, how they play, what a beginner should know (every
         40k faction, every Kill Team team — the roster from the Knowledge Base).
         Lore: the setting, the big events, the characters, the planets — answered in Deep
         Thought's voice, short by default, longer on request.
         Rules concepts: what a term means, how a mechanic works, in plain language (the
         glossary and concept chunks from the Knowledge Base).
         Units and models: what a unit is for and how it plays — but never its stats; those are on
         the datasheet, and the box says so.
         Browsable on the touchscreen (search box, faction tree, A–Z) or asked aloud through
         the mic.
         Works offline for everything in the local database; falls through to the cloud for the
         long tail, and caches the answer so the second ask is free and instant.

  Teacher — "teach us to play"

         Teach mode runs the first-game scripts (Kill Team first, then 40k Combat Patrol): the
         box narrates each step, shows the step on screen, explains each rule the first time it is
         needed, and waits for the players to say Next.

         Just-in-time rules: the first time a player shoots, charges, is injured, runs out of
         activations, etc., the box explains that rule once and never again that game.
         Rules lookup at any moment: press Ask, say "what does Lethal 5+ mean", get the
         answer in three seconds from the local glossary; harder questions go to the cloud.
         Worked examples on screen: the hit → wound → save → damage loop, the Kill Team
         block table, the strength-vs-toughness chart — as always-available reference cards.

  Referee — "run the game"

         Tracks the whole game state: players, battle round / turning point, phase, whose
         activation or turn, command points for both sides, victory points by category
         (Crit/Tac/Kill op; primary/secondary), units destroyed, time per turn.
         Announces every phase and prompts the right thing at the right time ("Command
         phase: gain a CP, then score" / "Order? Action?").
         Enforces the bookkeeping rules players forget: scoring caps, CP gain per turning
         point, the no-score-in-TP1 rule, kill grades, primary-op bonus maths.
         Big-number scoreboard on the touchscreen and on the Chromebook across the table
         (any browser on the Wi-Fi).
         Event log: every score, kill and phase change with a timestamp, so a disputed point
         can be rewound (double-tap the button, or Undo on screen) and the game can be
         reviewed afterwards.
         Timers: a soft per-turn clock with Deep Thought's remarks when it runs long.
         Modes: Teach (full narration), Reminder (phase prompts and tracking only), Silent
         scorer (screen only).

  Voice — "the Deep Thought personality"

         Every announcement, prompt and remark is spoken in the persona from Part 1 of the
         Knowledge Base, from a bank of pre-recorded lines with several variants per moment,
         so it never repeats itself within a game.
         New lines for unusual moments are written in-voice by the cloud, spoken, and cached.
         Hold the button to talk: the box listens while it is held, answers aloud and on screen
         when released.
         The dome ring is the box's "face": colour = round, pulse = active player, a flare for big
         rolls, a slow breathe when idle.

  2 — Hardware reality and the rules it forces
  The Pi 2 Model B is a 900 MHz quad-core 32-bit ARM with 1 GB of RAM, no GPU
  acceleration worth using from Python, and a 16 GB SD card. It can run a snappy
  touchscreen app, play audio, drive LEDs and talk to the network. It cannot run a language
  model, a speech recogniser of any quality, or a browser-based UI at a tolerable frame rate.
  Every design choice below follows from that.

  Design rules that follow:

     1. Offline first. Everything a game night needs works with the Wi-Fi down: tracking,
        teaching, the lines bank, the local encyclopedia. The cloud adds depth; it is never on
         the critical path. If the network is gone, the Ask button says so in character and falls
         back to local search.
     2. Pre-generate, then play. All known speech is made once (on your PC, by a script) and
         shipped as files. The Pi never synthesises speech.
     3. Cache everything from the cloud. Every answer and every generated line is stored by
         a hash of its request. Game night two is cheaper than game night one; by game night
         ten most questions never leave the box.
     4. One process, one loop. A single Python process with a 30 fps UI loop and a few
         background threads (audio, buttons, LEDs, network). No services talking over
         sockets, no containers; simple to debug over SSH.
     5. Budget: UI frame under 20 ms; button-to-sound under 150 ms; local lookup under 300
        ms; cloud answer 3–6 s with a "thinking" cue on the dome so the wait reads as
         character, not lag. RAM under 300 MB total.
     6. Degrade in character. Every failure mode (no Wi-Fi, no cloud key, mic missing, SD full)
        has a Deep Thought line and a screen message, never a traceback on the display.

  3 — Architecture




  process layout · 8 modules on the Pi, 5 things outside it

  One Python 3 process, started by systemd at boot, owning the screen. The game engine
  is the centre: it holds the session and emits events ( phase_start , activation , score ,
   kill , big_roll , timer_warn , game_over ). Everything else either feeds it (UI, button,
  plugins) or reacts to it (persona picks a line and plays it; the ring changes colour; the
  scoreboard server publishes the new state). No module calls the cloud except the cloud
  client, and nothing waits on it: a cloud request runs on a thread and posts a cloud_answer
  event when done.

  State machine (top level): BOOT → HOME → PICK_GAME → SETUP → PLAY ↔ (LOOKUP |
  ENCYCLOPEDIA | SCOREBOARD) → GAME_OVER → HOME , with SETTINGS and DICE reachable
  from HOME and PLAY. Inside PLAY the plugin owns a second machine (round → phase →
  step). The UI shows whichever screen matches the state; the button's meaning is set per
  state (Section 9).

  Threads: UI loop (main), audio, button, ring, mic, cloud worker, web server. All
  communicate through the event queue; shared state is the session object behind a lock,
  written only by the engine.

  5 — Game engine and plugins
  The session is one JSON-serialisable object, saved after every event so a power cut mid-
  game loses nothing:

     session = {
       game: "killteam", mode: "reminder", started: "2026-11-21T19:10",
         players: [{name: "Ethan", side: 0}, {name: "Trevor", side: 1}],
         round: 2, round_max: 4, phase: "firefight", active_side: 1,
         cp: [3, 4],
         vp: [{crit: 2, tac: 0, kill: 1, primary: "crit"}, {crit: 1, tac: 2, kill:
     0, primary: "tac"}],
       units: [{start: 6, lost: 1}, {start: 6, lost: 2}],
         activations_left: [3, 2],
         flags: {taught: ["shoot", "charge"], tp1_no_crit_warned: true},
         log: [{t: 812.4, ev: "score", side: 0, cat: "crit", delta: 1}, ...]
     }


  Undo pops the last log entry and replays the log from the last snapshot (snapshots every
  25 events), so any state is reconstructible and nothing is ever edited in place.

  Plugin interface ( games/base.py ). A game is a class with:

          phases(session) → the ordered phase list for the current round, each with {id,
         name, prompt_line, screen_numbers, buttons, auto_events} .

          on_event(session, event) → applies game rules: CP gain at phase start, scoring
         caps, kill-grade thresholds, the "no Crit Op in TP1" rule, end-of-game bonus maths.
         Returns follow-up events (e.g. score → score_capped if at cap).
          score_sheet(session) → the rows the end-of-round sheet shows and their caps.

          teach_script() → the ordered steps (Section 6) and the just-in-time trigger map.
          lines() → game-specific line moments on top of the shared persona bank ("Turning
         point two" vs "Battle round two").
          ring_scheme() → round colours and the pulse rule.

  Kill Team plugin ( games/killteam/ ): rounds = 4 turning points (2 in tutorial length);
  phases = Strategy (initiative roll-off, CP gain 1/1 in TP1 then 1 for initiative holder and 2 for
  the other, ready-up, ploys) → Firefight (alternating activations; the engine counts
  activations per side from the roster size, prompts Order? Action? in Teach, flags
  counteract when one side is out) → End (score sheet: Crit Op per mission card up to 6, Tac
  Op up to 6 once revealed, Kill Op by kill grades up to 6). Game over: primary-op bonus =
  ceil(score on primary / 2), max 3. Missions: the box stores only shape ("score for holding",
  "score for actions") and the per-round cap; the specific Crit Op card is the players'.

  40k plugin ( games/wh40k/ ): rounds = 5 battle rounds (3 in tutorial), two turns per round;
  phases per turn = Command (both gain 1 CP; battle-shock prompt for units under half;
  scoring prompt from round 2) → Movement → Shooting → Charge → Fight (with the "who
  fights next" alternation prompt) → end of turn. Score sheet: primary per the mission's cap,
  secondaries as free-text rows with their caps (drawn from a small table of the common
  2026–27 secondaries, editable), end-of-game points. Game sizes set the point budget
  shown on the Setup screen for reference only.

  Timers: per-turn and per-activation clocks run silently; at a configurable threshold
  (default 4 min per 40k phase, 90 s per KT activation) Deep Thought gets one dry line, then
  stays quiet.

  Validation the engine enforces (so the box never shows an impossible score): category
  caps, max CP (none in KT; 40k none but warns above 12), kills ≤ starting units, round ≤
  max, no scoring events in a phase that doesn't score. Anything else is the players'
  business.


  6 — Teach mode and rules lookup
  Teach scripts are the Part 4 / Part 8 tables from the Knowledge Base as data: teach.json
  per game, an ordered list of steps {id, title, text, line_id, card, wait_for} .
   wait_for is next (button or tap) or an event name. The engine runs the script as a
  cursor over the list; the Play screen keeps working underneath, so a Teach game is a
  tracked game — scores and phases are real.

  Just-in-time rules are a map trigger → step_id in the same file: first_shoot ,
   first_charge , first_fight , first_injury , out_of_activations , first_cp_spend ,
   first_battle_shock , first_fall_back , first_advance , round_2_scoring … Each
  fires once per session (tracked in session.flags.taught ), shows the card in the "i"
  ribbon, and reads it aloud. In Reminder mode they do not fire, but the same cards are one
  tap away under Lookup.

  Who tells the box the player shot? Mostly the players, through the bottom-row event
  buttons (Shoot, Charge, Fight, Unit down, Score) — in Teach mode these are prominent
  and the box asks "what are you doing?" after each activation starts; in Reminder mode
  only Score, Unit down and Next remain. This is deliberate: a tabletop box cannot see the
  table, so the players narrate the one word that matters and get a rule back.

  Rules lookup pipeline ( knowledge/ask.py ), same path whether the question is typed or
  spoken:

     1. Normalise: lowercase, strip filler ("what does", "mean"), expand known aliases ("KT" →
        Kill Team, "TP" → turning point, "invuln" → invulnerable save).
     2. Exact term match in the glossary (both games, current game first). Hit → answer in ≤
        300 ms.
     3. Full-text search over concepts + encyclopedia with FTS5, ranked; if the top hit scores
        above a threshold, show it with its heading as the answer and a "more" button.
     4. Answer cache: hash of the normalised question → a previous cloud answer, if any.
     5. Cloud (if enabled and online): send the question, the current game and phase, the top
         three local hits as context, and voice.txt ; get back a short answer in Deep
         Thought's voice; TTS it; cache text + audio. If offline: "That requires a thought larger
         than this room. Read me the card, or ask again when the network returns."
     6. Guardrail: every cloud answer about a specific unit, weapon or card ends with a line
        pointing to the card, because the box must not pretend to know current stats.

  Reference cards are static images (PNG, 800×480, generated on the PC from the KB
  tables) so they render instantly: KT shooting sequence, KT block table, KT fight sequence,
  KT scoring summary, 40k wound chart, 40k phase order, 40k scoring summary, the
  glossary A–Z.


  7 — Encyclopedia
  Scope, in layers:

     1. Teaching layer (local, authoritative for the box): the glossary, the concept chunks, the
        team and faction profiles — everything in the Knowledge Base doc. ~400 entries.
     2. Lore layer (local, summary depth): one entry each for the major factions, sub-factions,
        named characters, key events, worlds and organisations of the 40k setting — a 100–
        300 word original summary per entry, written in Deep Thought's register, with "read
        more in the cloud" for depth. Target ~800 entries at v1.0, grown over time.

     3. Cloud layer (on demand): anything else, answered by the model from its general
        knowledge, with the local entry as context when one exists. Cached forever once
         asked.

  Data model ( knowledge.db , SQLite with FTS5):

     entries(id, kind, game, title, aliases, body, source, updated)
        kind: glossary | concept | team | faction | unit_role | lore | card
     entries_fts(title, aliases, body)            -- full-text index
     answers(qhash, question, answer, audio_path, created, hits)              -- cloud cache


  Unit entries describe role and feel ("Intercessors: the standard Primaris line infantry;
  durable, flexible, the unit to learn the game with"), never stats, points or rules text. That
  keeps the box useful across balance updates and keeps it on the right side of Games
  Workshop's IP.

  Sources and licensing. Everything in the local database is written for this project —
  original summaries, not copied text. Research sources for writing them: the official
  Warhammer Community articles and free rules PDFs, Lexicanum and the Warhammer 40k
  Fandom wiki (both community wikis; useful as reference, but their text is under share-
  alike licences and is not copied in), Goonhammer and other reviews for playstyle framing.
  The box is a fan project for one living room; it never distributes rules text or datasheets,
  and the Settings screen says so.

  Browsing: the Encyclopedia screen is three tabs (Factions tree, Kill Teams roster, Lore A–
  Z) plus search; every entry has read aloud and more. In-game, Lookup searches the same
  database with the current game's entries ranked first.

  Growing it: tools/build_knowledge.py on the PC takes the Knowledge Base doc
  (exported as Markdown) plus lore/*.md files and rebuilds knowledge.db ; sync.sh
  pushes it. Adding an entry is adding a Markdown file with a front-matter header. The cloud
  cache is also mined: questions asked three or more times get promoted to a local entry on
  the next build.


  8 — Voice and persona
  One voice, chosen once. All speech — pre-made lines and cloud answers alike — uses the
  same TTS voice so Deep Thought sounds like one entity. Pick a deep, measured, slightly
  flat English voice from a cloud TTS service (OpenAI, ElevenLabs, Azure and Google all




                                                                                           Page 14 of 29
Deep Thought — Software Spec




  have candidates; we audition three with the same five lines and you choose). The voice id
  goes in config.toml and never changes casually, because every cached file would need
  regenerating.

  Pre-generation ( tools/build_audio.py , run on the PC): reads persona/lines.json
  (the Part 1 line bank, extended as we go) and every game's teach.json , sends each line
  to TTS once, stores audio/<line_id>.ogg (Opus, 24 kbps mono — ~1 MB per 10 minutes
  of speech), and writes a manifest with the text hash so a changed line regenerates and an
  unchanged one doesn't. Numbers and names: the digits 0–30, "to", "and", and each player
  name are generated as separate clips and stitched for score readouts ("seven" + "to" +
  "five"); it sounds slightly clipped, which suits the character.

  Line selection ( persona/lines.py ): each moment has 3–8 variants; the picker avoids the
  last two used in this session and weights by register. Registers: teach (patient, one quip
  at the end), normal , terse (phase names and numbers only). Verbosity in Settings maps
  to a register plus a probability that any optional remark plays at all (chatty 100 %, normal
  60 %, terse 15 %).

  Speech queue ( persona/speaker.py ): one speaker at a time; phase announcements
  interrupt remarks, remarks never interrupt teaching; a new Next press cuts the current
  line short with a 150 ms fade so the box never talks over an impatient table.

  Cloud-written lines: when an event has no bank line (a 14-point swing, a game decided on
  a tie-break, a fourth undo in a row) the engine asks the cloud for one line in-voice, speaks
  it, and files it under that moment in the cache so the bank grows. Rate-limited to one per
  minute so the box doesn't get chatty on someone else's dime.

  Mic and speech-to-text: hold the button; arecord captures 16 kHz mono to a temp WAV
  while the ring shows the listening pattern; release stops it. Clips under 0.6 s are discarded
  as accidental. The clip goes to a cloud STT endpoint (Whisper-class); the transcript shows
  on screen for a second ("You asked: …") so a misheard question can be cancelled with a
  tap, then runs the lookup pipeline. Offline: the box says it cannot hear without the
  network and opens the typed Lookup instead.

  9 — Hardware services
  The button ( hw/button.py , GPIO 17 with internal pull-up, 30 ms debounce). The classifier
  turns raw edges into three gestures: press (down and up within 600 ms, no second press
  within 350 ms), hold (down for 600 ms+; emits hold_start at 600 ms and hold_end on
  release), double (two presses within 350 ms). The meaning is set by the current screen
  (Section 4 table); the classifier never knows what it means. A 5-second hold on the Home
  screen opens the shutdown prompt. The button's own LED is on a separate GPIO (not 5 V-
  always-on as in the first wiring plan — with one button we can afford to drive it): steady =
  on; slow pulse = "your move"; bright = listening; off = asleep.

  The ring ( hw/ring.py , 16 × WS2812 on SPI MOSI, brightness capped at 50 %). An
  animation thread at 30 fps runs named behaviours; the engine only ever names one:

  10 — Cloud service
  Three calls, one client ( cloud/client.py ), all over HTTPS with a 12-second timeout and
  one retry:

     1. STT: POST the WAV, get a transcript. (Whisper-class API; ~$0.006 per minute of audio;
        a question is 3–5 seconds.)
     2. Answer: POST to the Claude API with voice.txt as the system prompt, plus: game,
        phase, the player's question, the top three local knowledge hits as context, and the
        last two Q&A turns so follow-ups work ("and what about on Conceal?"). Ask for ≤ 4
        sentences. Use the smallest model that keeps the voice — Haiku-class — for rules and
        quips; Sonnet-class only for lore "tell me more" requests. A typical answer is ~1,500
         input tokens and ~120 output: well under a cent.
     3. TTS: POST the answer text, get audio, save as OGG under the cache. (~$0.015 per
        1,000 characters at typical rates.)

  Rough cost per game night: 20 spoken questions + 5 cloud-written lines ≈ $0.50 the first
  night, trending toward zero as the cache fills. A monthly spend cap (default $5) is
  enforced client-side from the logged usage; past it, the box says so and goes local-only.
  Settings shows this month's spend.

  Keys live in config.toml with mode 600, never in the repo. Privacy: only the question
  audio/text, game, phase and player first names leave the box; nothing else is logged
  upstream.

  Offline behaviour, in order: Wi-Fi check at boot and every minute; if down, Ask-by-voice is
  disabled with a line, typed Lookup stays local, and every cloud-dependent button shows a
  small cloud-off glyph. Nothing blocks, nothing retries forever.

  11 — Chromebook scoreboard
  A Flask thread serves two things on port 8080: GET /state.json (the session, minus the
  log) and GET / (one static HTML page with a few lines of JavaScript that polls
   /state.json once a second). Open http://deepthought.local:8080 in the
  Chromebook's browser, press F11, stand it at the end of the table. It shows: both players'
  names and VP in very large type, CP, round and phase, the kill-grade bar (KT) or
  secondaries (40k), and a quiet line of the last Deep Thought remark. Read-only by design;
  all input stays on the box. Any phone on the Wi-Fi can open the same page.

  Later option: a second route, /control , protected by a PIN shown on the box's Settings
  screen, for adjusting scores from the Chromebook — useful when the box ends up at the

  14 — Honest limits and open decisions
  Limits that won't change on a Pi 2:

         It cannot see the table. Every game event it tracks is one the players tell it (a button, a
         tap, a word). The design makes that one tap, but it is still a tap.
         It cannot think without the network. Local = everything scripted and everything in the
         database; cloud = everything else. The persona sounds the same either way, which is
         the point of pre-generating.
         It cannot be the rulebook. It teaches the system and reads the glossary; stats, points
         and card text stay on the cards. This is both a legal line and a practical one — the box
         doesn't go stale every balance update.
         Voice recognition is push-to-talk, not always-listening. No wake word (not enough
         CPU, and nobody wants a mic open all night anyway).
         1 GB of RAM means one game at a time and no browser-based anything.

  Decisions to make before v0.3:

     1. The voice. Audition three; I'll prepare the same five lines in each.
     2. How chatty by default. Start on normal (60 % of optional remarks) and tune at the
        table.
     3. Player names. Pre-record a dozen common ones plus everyone in the group, or always
        use "player one / player two"? Pre-recording the group's names is the better
        experience and takes one build.
     4. Button LED. Driven from a GPIO (recommended, lets it pulse) means one extra jumper
        vs the always-on 5 V in the current wiring table; I'll update the assembly guide's Part 7
        either way, since it still lists four buttons.

  Decisions that can wait: cloud provider for STT/TTS (any works; cost differences are
  cents), whether to add the /control route, how far the lore layer grows, and when the Pi
  5 upgrade happens — the cloud client is the only module that would change.

  15.4 How a question gets answered now

         reply, not the excerpt.
     6. If search finds nothing, the box says so and answers from the knowledge database,

  16.6 Link to the fight tracker

  that leaves a character at 0 with leftover damage equal to their maximum is flagged as
  instant death, for the DM to confirm.

  A long rest restores hit points and slots. Under 2014 rules it returns half the character's hit
  dice; under 2024 rules, all of them.
